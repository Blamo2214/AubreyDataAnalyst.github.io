Oil Market Report — October 8

Trump rules out pre-midterm strikes against Iran, triggering a sharp crude selloff, while diesel markets remain exceptionally tight, Gulf of Mexico production shut-ins surge, and regional military tensions continue to escalate.

Major Developments

  • Trump rules out military strikes before the midterms: Following reports that the Pentagon had instructed CENTCOM to finalize preparations for renewed major combat operations against Iran, President Trump announced on Truth Social that the United States would not attack Iran before the November 3 midterm elections. He characterized ongoing discussions with Tehran as productive, maintained that the U.S. blockade would remain in effect, and repeated claims that Iran had been militarily and economically devastated. The announcement triggered an immediate selloff in crude futures. Source: Axios
  • Iran rejects uranium enrichment concessions: Mohammad Eslami, head of Iran's Atomic Energy Organization, reiterated that Iran would neither abandon uranium enrichment nor surrender its uranium stockpiles. The statement highlighted the continuing distance between Tehran's publicly stated position and Washington's demands for nuclear concessions. Source: Reuters / Arab News
  • U.S. announces additional Iran-related sanctions: The Treasury Department imposed new sanctions targeting networks and 17 vessels involved in transporting Iranian crude oil, petroleum products, and petrochemicals. The measures target Iran's shadow fleet and associated revenue networks, arriving the same day Trump publicly emphasized productive negotiations and a pause in military escalation. Source: Reuters
  • Hurricane Isaias shuts in nearly two-thirds of Gulf oil production: The Marine Minerals Administration reported that approximately 1,282,879 barrels per day of Gulf of Mexico oil production, representing 62.89% of current offshore output, had been shut in. Natural gas shut-ins reached 57.35%, while 121 production platforms and five rigs were evacuated. Production may recover after the storm passes, depending on damage assessments and safety inspections. Source: Marine Minerals Administration
  • Houthis launch additional attacks against Saudi Arabia: Houthi forces claimed further ballistic and cruise missile attacks targeting Saudi airports and military facilities, including Riyadh's King Khalid International Airport. Saudi-led coalition forces reported intercepting several missiles. The attacks disrupted aviation and intensified concerns about the expanding conflict near the Red Sea and Bab el-Mandeb shipping corridor. Source: Associated Press
  • Kpler analyst raises possibility of substantial Iranian transit payments: Michelle Brohard, a senior Kpler analyst, suggested that some Gulf producers may be privately negotiating safe passage through Hormuz by paying Iran as much as 10–20% of cargo value. For a two-million-barrel VLCC carrying $100 crude, that would represent approximately $20–40 million per shipment. These arrangements remain speculative and have not been independently established. Source: Al Jazeera

Crude futures rallied steadily overnight following reports that the United States was preparing for possible renewed military operations against Iran. November WTI approached $90 around 1 a.m. Central time before advancing above $92 by morning and briefly touching approximately $93. Brent reached roughly $105 per barrel. The rally developed gradually rather than through a single sharp price spike.

Refined products were particularly strong, with November ULSD advancing approximately $0.21 per gallon toward $4.08, while gasoline gained roughly $0.10 toward $3.34. Diesel crack spreads widened by approximately $5 per barrel to $111–112, while gasoline cracks increased only modestly to approximately $48. The divergence reflected continued pressure in the distillate market beyond the broader rise in crude prices.

Following Trump's announcement ruling out military strikes before the midterms, WTI fell rapidly from nearly $93 to approximately $90.50 before recovering toward $91. Brent similarly retreated from its morning highs. Diesel prices, however, remained substantially higher on the day, with crack spreads still near $112 per barrel. The sharp reaction in crude contrasted with the persistence of the refined-product premium.

Agitlytics' calculations using EIA petroleum supply data indicate that U.S. diesel exports rose to approximately 33.3% of domestic production in the latest reporting period. This illustrates the extent to which international distillate demand continues drawing on U.S. production. Elevated global diesel prices, particularly in Europe, continue transmitting supply pressure into the domestic market through export economics.

Meanwhile, Hurricane Isaias introduced a major temporary disruption to domestic crude production. Offshore Gulf oil shut-ins expanded to approximately 1.28 million barrels per day, with nearly 63% of current production offline. Although substantial production may return after the storm passes, the interruption represents an additional physical supply constraint at a time of considerable global petroleum-market uncertainty.

Agitlytics Analysis

Today's market action was an interesting continuation of yesterday's developments. Following reporting from The Atlantic and Axios that the administration was preparing for potential strikes against Iran before the midterms, crude prices climbed steadily overnight. WTI approached $90 around 1 a.m. and continued drifting higher into the morning, eventually touching nearly $93, while Brent reached approximately $105. There wasn't one enormous spike that explained the entire move. Instead, the market appeared to be gradually pricing in an increased likelihood of escalation.

The additional Houthi attacks against Saudi Arabia certainly didn't help matters. We have ballistic missiles targeting airports, continued fighting around Yemen, and increasing threats to regional transportation infrastructure. All of this is happening while the petroleum market is already struggling with extraordinarily high freight costs, persistent attacks on tankers, and severe refined-product tightness. So while I think the overnight rally was largely a response to the increased possibility of U.S. strikes against Iran, there were additional developments reinforcing the broader supply-risk premium.

Then Trump posted on Truth Social that the United States would not attack Iran before November 3, and crude immediately ripped lower. WTI fell from nearly $93 to approximately $90.50 before recovering toward $91. That was an extraordinary reaction to a single statement, particularly after the steady overnight advance. But what interested me even more was what didn't happen: diesel crack spreads remained near $112 per barrel.

Trump's Truth Social post this morning appears to be another attempt at price jawboning. Following yesterday's reporting from The Atlantic and Axios that the administration was preparing for potential strikes against Iran before the midterms, crude prices climbed steadily overnight. WTI approached $93 this morning, and I suspect Trump wasn't particularly pleased with that development. Now he's explicitly ruling out strikes before November 3, and crude immediately sells off.

But I think the administration continues to misunderstand where the real political pressure is coming from. The average midterm voter isn't watching WTI or Brent futures. They're watching what they're paying at the pump, and that's where the bottleneck remains. Gasoline and diesel markets are still extraordinarily tight, particularly diesel. WTI could fall another $15 per barrel, but that doesn't necessarily resolve the underlying refined-product shortage. Diesel crack spreads are still around $112 per barrel, even after this morning's sharp decline in crude.

Yesterday's EIA figures also showed that U.S. diesel exports increased to approximately 33.3% of domestic production, according to our Agitlytics calculations. With no export ban in place, global demand continues pulling American diesel into international markets, particularly given the extraordinary tightness in Europe. That global shortage is directly affecting domestic prices, and none of the administration's rhetoric about defeating Iran or bringing crude prices down fundamentally changes that.

This also makes yesterday's developments even more difficult to interpret. One possible explanation for the administration considering strikes before the midterms was that it had recognized that waiting wasn't necessarily going to improve the refined-product situation. If diesel and gasoline markets weren't going to loosen substantially before November anyway, perhaps the thinking was to simply rip the Band-Aid off and proceed with whatever military objectives they believed they could accomplish in Iran.

But now Trump appears to be reversing course again, explicitly ruling out strikes before the election. How much of that represents an actual strategic decision, and how much is simply another attempt to push crude prices lower through headlines, remains unclear.

I wouldn't be surprised if we see another contradictory announcement after tomorrow's market close. That's speculative, of course, but the pattern of increasingly inconsistent statements makes it difficult to determine which announcements reflect genuine policy decisions and which are primarily intended to influence market expectations.

The larger problem remains unchanged: the administration can temporarily push crude prices lower with reassuring headlines, but it cannot talk its way out of a physical shortage of refined petroleum products. Until those supply constraints begin to ease, lower crude prices alone are unlikely to provide the relief at the pump that the administration appears to be seeking.

Another interesting development is the timing of Trump's announcement relative to statements coming out of Iran. Iran's nuclear leadership reiterated that the country would neither abandon uranium enrichment nor surrender its uranium stockpiles, even as Trump was publicly describing productive negotiations and ruling out additional strikes before the midterms.

This comes after a substantial number of reported maritime attacks over the past week, including yesterday's tanker strike off Qatar. Meanwhile, there is speculation that Iran may be collecting transit fees amounting to 10–20% of cargo value from vessels passing through Hormuz. That remains unconfirmed, but the broader point is that Iran appears to retain considerable leverage over shipping through the Strait.

The Kpler theory is particularly interesting because it fits with the argument we've been making for several days. Recovering crude flows do not necessarily mean Iran has lost control of the Strait. In fact, it may be that oil is flowing precisely because exporters are finding increasingly expensive ways to secure passage. If some Gulf producers are privately paying Tehran substantial sums to move their cargoes, then the recovery in export volumes could coexist with considerable Iranian leverage over shipping.

To put the proposed figures in perspective, a two-million-barrel VLCC carrying $100 crude would represent approximately $200 million in cargo value. A 10–20% payment would therefore amount to $20–40 million for a single tanker. That would be an extraordinary cost, and potentially an enormous source of revenue for Iran if arrangements of that magnitude were occurring regularly. Again, these are speculative estimates, not confirmed transactions, but the implications are worth considering.

So what exactly is the administration's position here? Iran is openly refusing to concede on uranium enrichment, tanker attacks are continuing, and Tehran may even be extracting substantial payments from vessels seeking safe passage. Yet Trump responds by explicitly ruling out additional strikes before the midterms.

Assuming he's telling the truth, it increasingly appears that the administration feels politically constrained by the approaching election. Iran can continue threatening shipping, refusing nuclear concessions, and asserting control over the Strait, while Washington effectively announces that it will not escalate militarily before November 3.

Then, adding to the confusion, the Treasury Department announced additional Iran-related sanctions only about an hour after Trump's statement. Sanctions and negotiations are not inherently contradictory, of course, and economic pressure can be part of a negotiating strategy. But in the context of everything else we've seen over the past 24 hours, the sequence is remarkable. We went from reports that CENTCOM was preparing for major combat operations, to Trump ruling out military action before the election, to another round of economic sanctions targeting Iran's petroleum revenue networks.

Meanwhile, we also have a major domestic supply disruption developing in the Gulf of Mexico. Nearly 63% of offshore oil production has been shut in ahead of Hurricane Isaias, amounting to approximately 1.28 million barrels per day. This is likely to be temporary, assuming the storm passes without significant infrastructure damage, but it's another example of how multiple disruptions are occurring simultaneously across the petroleum system. Even as the administration attempts to cool prices through rhetoric, there are real physical supply constraints that simply cannot be resolved through announcements.

Alternatively, Trump's statement could simply be another attempt to jawbone crude prices lower before reversing course. That's increasingly where my thinking is heading. I wouldn't be surprised if Trump makes another aggressive or contradictory announcement after tomorrow's market close, once the immediate political and market consequences are easier to manage.

Of course, that's speculation. But given the conflicting statements and reports we've seen over the past 24 hours, it's becoming increasingly difficult to distinguish genuine strategic decisions from announcements intended primarily to influence oil prices.

As things currently stand, the fundamental distinction remains between crude prices reacting to political headlines and refined-product markets responding to persistent physical tightness. Trump can announce that Iran has been defeated, that shipping through Hormuz is reaching record volumes, or that the United States will refrain from military action until after the election. Those statements can move crude futures almost immediately. But they do not automatically restore refining capacity, reduce the cost of transporting petroleum, rebuild diesel inventories, or eliminate the international competition for available refined products.

And that's ultimately why I remain skeptical that these repeated attempts to push crude prices lower will translate into meaningful relief for consumers. The underlying petroleum system remains under extraordinary pressure, and the market's continuing willingness to pay more than $110 per barrel in diesel crack spreads suggests that the refined-product crisis is nowhere near resolved.

The disruption adds another near-term supply concern, although its duration remains uncertain.

Meanwhile, U.S. officials continued arguing that Iran had lost control of the Strait of Hormuz, despite ongoing tanker attacks and Iranian statements asserting continued control over shipping routes. Refined Products and Market Structure

Refined-product markets opened substantially stronger, with November diesel crack spreads approaching $108 per barrel and gasoline cracks near $50. The November diesel premium over gasoline widened to approximately $1.38 per gallon, compared with roughly $1.18 during the recent compression. The strength in diesel pricing continued to highlight the disparity between crude availability and refined-product supply.

Following the IEA's announcement, November WTI declined toward $88, Brent slipped below $100, and diesel and gasoline crack spreads retreated to approximately $104 and $47 per barrel, respectively. The move reflected a sharp reduction in immediate price pressure, although the underlying refined-product supply constraints remained unresolved.

A notable divergence emerged across the crude futures curve. While November 2026 crude declined approximately $1 and December lost roughly $0.70, contracts from March through December 2027 traded higher, averaging gains of approximately $0.20. July 2027 was particularly strong, gaining around $0.34. This suggests the day's selling was concentrated in nearby contracts rather than reflecting an equivalent decline in longer-term pricing expectations.

Following The Atlantic's afternoon reporting on potential U.S. strikes, November WTI rebounded rapidly from approximately $88.20 to $88.60. Diesel crack spreads recovered toward $107, while gasoline cracks remained comparatively subdued near $47. The reaction illustrated how quickly geopolitical headlines continued to influence petroleum prices.

Agitlytics Analysis

Today's price action was particularly interesting because it showed two competing forces operating in the market. On one hand, we have governments attempting to bring prices down through strategic reserve releases, improving crude flows, and repeated statements that the situation in the Strait of Hormuz is coming under control. On the other hand, we continue to see tanker attacks, extremely tight refined-product markets, and increasingly serious indications that the United States may be preparing to escalate the war again.

The IEA announcement was the most obvious bearish development during the regular trading session. Brent slipped below $100, November WTI fell toward $88, and the diesel and gasoline cracks surrendered some of their overnight gains. But I think it's important to distinguish between actually introducing new supply and accelerating barrels that were already committed to the market. Much of the roughly 100 million barrels being discussed appears to be previously pledged emergency inventory that simply hasn't been delivered yet. That doesn't mean accelerating those barrels is meaningless, particularly if diesel stocks are prioritized, but it is a very different development from announcing an entirely new 100-million-barrel intervention.

My initial interpretation was that this was another attempt at price jawboning. Governments are trying to cool prices and tensions, and the market is responding to the headlines. Whether that response is sustainable is another question. The IEA itself is acknowledging that diesel supplies remain exceptionally tight, which is precisely the problem we've been discussing over the past several days. Recovering crude flows do not necessarily resolve disruptions to refining, transportation, and refined-product distribution.

The EIA inventory report reinforced some of that concern. Commercial crude inventories declined by 3.2 million barrels, while distillate inventories were essentially unchanged despite refinery utilization reaching 92.7%. That is particularly interesting given the strength we've been seeing in diesel crack spreads. Refineries are running at relatively high utilization, yet we're not seeing meaningful rebuilding of distillate inventories. In fact, reported distillate stocks remain substantially below their seasonal five-year average. It seems increasingly difficult to argue that the refined-product problem is simply going to disappear because additional crude is moving through the Strait.

Another development I found particularly interesting was the behavior of the futures curve. While November and December 2026 crude contracts were down considerably, contracts further out in 2027 were actually gaining value. The front of the curve was reacting to immediate easing headlines, but the back of the curve wasn't showing the same degree of optimism.

I think one possible interpretation is that the market is beginning to price a more prolonged disruption to the petroleum system, even if crude flows recover in the near term. Elevated transportation costs, insurance, freight, storage, and the continued tightness in refined products could become part of a more persistent cost structure. I wouldn't necessarily attribute the entire move in the back of the curve to transportation costs, but the divergence is consistent with the possibility that the market is becoming less confident in a quick return to prewar conditions. In other words, the immediate crisis premium might be easing without the market concluding that the broader problem has been solved.

The situation in Hormuz is another reason I'm skeptical of the repeated claims that shipping conditions are normalizing. The administration continues pointing to recovering export volumes as evidence that Iran has lost control of the Strait. But that doesn't necessarily follow. Tanker attacks have accelerated even as traffic has recovered, and Iranian officials continue to insist that they retain the ability to block routes they consider unauthorized.

There is also the possibility, raised by analysts at Kpler, that at least some vessels are passing safely because arrangements are being made with Iran, potentially involving transit payments. That remains speculative, and I don't think we can treat it as an established fact. But it would help explain how recovering flows and continued Iranian control could coexist. More fundamentally, the ability to strike vessels, threaten particular routes, and impose substantial costs on maritime traffic is itself evidence of continuing coercive capability. The fact that more oil is moving doesn't necessarily mean Iran has lost that capability.

Today's additional tanker strike off Qatar only reinforces that concern. The UKMTO report described a tanker being hit by multiple projectiles, with casualties reported. This happened well inside the Persian Gulf, rather than simply at the narrowest point of the Hormuz transit corridor. Once again, the physical security situation appears considerably more complicated than the administration's public statements would suggest.

The afternoon and evening developments made the geopolitical picture even more concerning. The Atlantic reported that Trump was considering strikes against Iran before the midterms, and oil immediately recovered some of its losses. Then Axios reported that the Pentagon had already instructed CENTCOM to finalize preparations for potentially resuming major combat operations. Those reports don't establish that strikes will happen, but they make the possibility of renewed escalation much more concrete.

What stands out to me is how quickly the market responded. It took a combination of strategic reserve announcements, improving-flow headlines, and repeated assurances of de-escalation to push November WTI back toward $88. Yet one report about possible renewed military action produced an immediate upward reaction. The move wasn't enormous, but it demonstrated how sensitive the market remains to the possibility that the conflict could intensify.

This brings me back to the broader argument I've been making: crude availability is only one part of the problem. The costs and risks associated with transporting, refining, and distributing petroleum remain elevated, and the market continues to respond sharply to any suggestion that those disruptions could worsen. Even if more barrels are successfully moving through Hormuz, that does not mean the system transporting them has returned to anything resembling normal conditions.

Beyond the market itself, today's developments have also forced me to reconsider my interpretation of the administration's political strategy.

I had been working under the assumption that Trump intended to postpone any major escalation with Iran until after the midterms, while spending the intervening weeks trying to ease tensions and bring energy prices down. That seemed to provide some logic to the administration’s moves: proposing and then abandoning a diesel export ban, lifting restrictions on red-dyed diesel, repeatedly talking about falling oil prices, and increasingly declaring Iran defeated.

But the developments today make that interpretation much harder to sustain. This morning, Trump and Bessent were effectively arguing that Iran was no longer the priority because the U.S. had regained control of the Strait of Hormuz. That claim itself is difficult to reconcile with what is actually happening: tanker traffic has recovered, but attacks on vessels have accelerated, reaching their highest rate of the war, while Iran continues to insist that it retains the ability to control passage through the Strait.

Then, only hours later, The Atlantic reported that the administration is seriously considering additional strikes against Iran before the midterms. That creates an obvious contradiction. If Iran has been defeated, the Strait is under U.S. control, and Iran is no longer the priority, why escalate the war now? If those claims were remotely true, they would seemingly provide the administration with exactly the offramp it needs.

I have tried to give the administration the benefit of the doubt and identify some coherent strategy connecting these individual moves and headlines. I am a political and ideological opponent of Trump, so simply concluding that the administration has no idea what it is doing feels almost too easy—frankly, like a lazy explanation. But I increasingly feel as though I have exhausted the alternatives. There does not appear to be a consistent path forward. The policy instead looks increasingly reactive: attempting one measure, abandoning it, declaring success, and then contemplating an escalation that directly contradicts the rationale offered only hours earlier.

  • Conflict around Yemen, Saudi Arabia, and the Red Sea remained another source of regional petroleum risk, while the wider Russia–Ukraine energy conflict continued in parallel.
  • President Trump said the United States still has to "finish up" in Iran and that the remaining question is how, again leaving open the possibility of further U.S. escalation.

Refined Products & Physical Market

Refined products reversed their early weakness alongside crude. U.S. diesel was down roughly 15 cents per gallon during the morning selloff before recovering, while gasoline and diesel ultimately moved approximately 4 cents and 3 cents higher, respectively. Diesel cracks fell into the $97-per-barrel range before recovering to roughly $102, while gasoline cracks moved toward approximately $48.

The broader physical-market problem increasingly extends beyond crude availability itself. Russian refinery and export disruptions, tight distillate availability, extraordinary tanker rates, insurance costs, security requirements, longer voyages, and increasingly complicated shipping arrangements continue to impose enormous costs on the global petroleum system even as Middle Eastern crude exports recover.

Agitlytics Analysis

The question surrounding the recent recovery in Middle Eastern oil flows is increasingly being framed backwards. If oil is flowing out of the Strait of Hormuz again, why is WTI still around $90 and Brent above $100? The reality is that oil is not $100 despite flowing through Hormuz. To a significant extent, oil is flowing through Hormuz because it is $100. That is effectively what it now costs to compensate the market for the extraordinary risk and expense required to move those barrels.

The scale of those costs is becoming increasingly visible. Tanker captains are reportedly being offered compensation approaching $100,000 per month and bonuses of nearly $50,000 for individual Hormuz voyages. That comes on top of extraordinary tanker rates, insurance premiums, military escorts, security costs, longer voyages, and increasingly complicated methods of moving crude around the disruption. Recovering exports therefore should not be confused with normalization. The market has simply found increasingly expensive ways to move oil through and around the disruption.

The same problem is visible in refined products. The bottleneck is increasingly not simply whether enough crude exists globally, but whether the world has sufficient functioning refining capacity and logistical capacity to turn that crude into products and deliver them where they are needed. Russia has become particularly important to that story as attacks continue to pressure its refining and export infrastructure. Meanwhile, diesel remains extraordinarily tight. Today's move in diesel cracks — roughly $100 to $97 and then back toward $102 — demonstrates how quickly apparent relief continues to disappear.

Hormuz itself also appears to be moving in the wrong direction. Strikes have increased as traffic through the Strait has recovered, and those attacks have so far gone largely unanswered by the United States. That fits the question raised earlier in this reporting: whether Iran is simply launching more attacks, whether U.S. defenses are becoming less effective, or whether Tehran has deliberately shifted from maintaining the threat of disruption toward actually imposing greater physical costs as shipping recovered. Whatever the explanation, the market implication is similar. More crude is moving, but the Strait has not become safe.

At the same time, this is not a single-front petroleum crisis. Conflict involving Yemen and Saudi Arabia continues around another critical shipping corridor, while Russia and Ukraine continue a medium, constant burn against energy and refining infrastructure. Rather than one overwhelming supply shock, several simultaneous conflicts are applying pressure to different parts of the global petroleum system.

This makes it increasingly difficult for me to identify a convincing short-term pathway toward materially cheaper crude. Genuine de-escalation with Iran and normalization of Hormuz would obviously change the equation. Another, more unusual bear case would be the United States effectively admitting defeat, withdrawing from the confrontation, and accepting Iranian control of the Strait. Even if Iran subsequently imposed some form of transit fee, that cost could still be considerably smaller than the risk premium currently being imposed through attacks, military escorts, insurance, freight, and uncertainty.

That does not appear to be the direction Washington is moving. Trump said today that the United States still has to "finish up" in Iran and that the question is how, again describing a choice between doing it the nice way or the not-so-nice way. Combined with the continuing U.S. military buildup in the region, it is difficult to view a simple American withdrawal as the base case.

Brent may therefore be giving a better picture of the actual global petroleum market than WTI. Brent trading around $101 while WTI remains around $90 reflects how much tighter the overseas physical market has become. From my perspective, that divergence increasingly looks less like Brent being incorrectly expensive and more like WTI potentially being mispriced to the downside relative to the global barrel. Tightness in the international market is not solved simply because crude remains comparatively available inside the United States.

Today's price action reinforces that view. WTI fell from the high $89s overnight into the $86 handle during the morning before recovering almost the entire move and returning toward $90. Diesel cracks performed essentially the same round trip. The market continues to sell improved-flow, reserve-release, and de-escalation headlines, but it has repeatedly struggled to keep crude down once those initial reactions run their course.

For now, I continue to view roughly $88 as a working short-term floor for November WTI rather than a hard technical level. The important feature is the asymmetry. A sustained move materially below that area probably requires something structural to change — genuine de-escalation, substantially safer shipping, restored refining capacity, or a durable improvement in physical flows and logistics. Another move several dollars higher may require nothing more than a single additional disruption. As things currently stand, it remains difficult to see the balance of pressure pointing anywhere but upward.
